Volunteer
Join Our Team as a Volunteer for the Legal Design Summit in Helsinki September 2025!
What?
Welcome to the Legal Design Summit, where innovation, collaboration, and cutting-edge ideas collide! Our mission is to create a vibrant platform where forward-thinking professionals can exchange knowledge and expertise on law, design, and digital services.
As we embark on this transformative journey, we need an incredible team of volunteers to make it all happen! Volunteering with us means having a front-row seat to the creation of the world's leading legal design event. Whether you're new to legal design or an expert in the field, this is your chance to immerse yourself in an incredible global network of legal design!
A Place for Everyone!
No matter your background, expertise, or professional field, we want you! You don't need to be a lawyer or a legal student to be a part of this amazing community, all people irrespective of their background are welcome. What is BrainFactory?
BrainFactory is a one-day event with highly interactive, immersive, and inspiring hands-on workshops, brainstorming sessions, and protoyping exercises.
For 2025, we're reimagining BrainFactory into interactive workshops where you not only get to experience legal design, you get to dive into a specific area of legal design. Our BrainFactory team together with our Partners are building workshops that arise from real life examples from the cross-sections of law and design thinking.
Brains on for a full day of legal design! You'll attend two workshops - one in the morning and one in the afternoon - where you'll get to know other interested and like-minded legal professionals and dive into the topics at hand. Are you new to legal design? Don't worry - we've got you covered. We have a specific workshop track to get your feet wet in legal design and still pick up more tips and tricks if you have some experience.

Think design is just for creatives? Think again. Travel & Accommodation
Plan your trip to Helsinki for the Legal Design Summit 2025
Summit venue: 5 Aleksanterinkatu, Helsinki
Summit Venue
The Legal Design Summit 2025 will take place at the University of Helsinki’s main building at 5 Aleksanterinkatu, 00100 Helsinki, Finland . The venue is in the heart of the city centre, next to Senate Square and the Helsinki Cathedral. You’ll find a fully accessible entrance on Aleksanterinkatu and the beautiful Great Hall inside.
Use tram 2, 4 or 7 to get to the venue – the Senaatintori stop is right around the corner. Helsinki Central Station is a short 7‑minute walk away.
View in Google Maps Tourist Attractions and Summit venue
Getting There
From the airport: Helsinki Airport (HEL) is about 30 minutes from the city centre. Take the I or P train directly from the airport to Helsinki Central Station, then walk or hop on tram 2, 4 or 7 to reach the venue. Taxis and airport buses are also available outside the terminal.
By train: Long‑distance trains arrive at Helsinki Central Station. From there, it’s a short walk along Keskuskatu to Aleksanterinkatu, or you can take the trams mentioned above.
By bus/tram: The HSL network covers the entire Helsinki region. You can purchase day tickets via the HSL app. Tram lines 2 and 4 stop at Senate Square near the venue, and numerous buses serve nearby stops.
Accommodation
We recommend booking your stay early. Here are a few options within walking distance of the Summit venue:

Explore Helsinki
While you’re here, make time to discover the city’s highlights:
Helsinki Cathedral & Senate Square
The neoclassical cathedral is one of Finland’s most photographed landmarks and sits right next to the summit venue.
Suomenlinna Sea Fortress
A UNESCO World Heritage site just a 15‑minute ferry ride from the Market Square. Explore historic fortifications and enjoy seaside views.
Oodi Central Library
A modern architectural gem opposite the Parliament House. Grab a coffee, browse the library or simply admire the wooden wave‑like design.
Design District & Market Square
Wander through boutique shops and galleries in the Design District, then sample local delicacies at the Market Square.
Travel Tips
Weather: Late autumn in Helsinki can be chilly (0–5 °C) with occasional rain or snow. Bring warm layers and waterproof shoes.
Currency: Finland uses the Euro (€). Credit and debit cards are widely accepted, and contactless payment is common.
Language: Finnish and Swedish are the official languages, but English is widely spoken, especially in the city centre.
Emergency number: Dial 112 for police, fire or medical emergencies.
Public transport: the HSL app for real‑time tram, bus and metro timetables and to purchase tickets on the go.

By participating in the Legal Design Summit, you agree to abide by this Code of Conduct.
Our Values
Legal Design Summit is committed to the following values:
Selflessness
We believe in supporting each other in achieving our shared goals. We see each other and the world with empathy, and never put personal gain before the interests of the community.
Inclusion
We create a space where everyone feels welcome and valued, and where diversity and different perspectives are celebrated. To support this, LDS is accessible to everyone through free tickets.
Courage
Learning, imagination and reaching for the impossible is in our backbone. We are curious about the world and passionate about improving it. When faced with a problem to solve, we ask: how hard can it be?
Authenticity
We are a movement dedicated to making a difference through creative design-thinking. We strive to make an impact by showing up as our authentic selves, while remaining honest, open and true to our core values.
😊 Expected Behaviour
Treat everyone with kindness, courtesy, and empathy.
Actively work to create a welcoming environment for people of all backgrounds, identities, and abilities.
Keep an open mind.
Work together constructively and supportively—helping others and being open to receiving help.
Follow all instructions and rules provided by event organizers, including being mindful of the event space.
❌ Unacceptable Behaviour
Harassment: Any form of verbal, physical, or written abuse, intimidation, threats, or unwelcome advances.
Discrimination: Any form of prejudice or discrimination based on race, ethnicity, religion, gender, gender identity, sexual orientation, age, disability or any other personal characteristic.
Bullying: Repeated or persistent negative behaviour that is intended to intimidate, humiliate, or demean another person.
Disruption: Any behaviour that disrupts the event or creates a hostile environment.
Theft or Damage: Stealing or damaging property belonging to the organization, other participants, or the venue.
Who to contact?
If you experience or witness any behavior that violates this Code of Conduct, please report it immediately to the Vice Chair of the Board (Sade Mäntylä). All reports will be treated confidentially to the extent possible. Legal Design Summit is committed to investigating all reports of Code of Conduct violations promptly and fairly. Depending on the severity of the violation, we may take action, such as issuing a warning, removal from current and future events or when necessary, contacting relevant authorities.
By participating in the 2025 Legal Design Summit, you acknowledge that you have read, understood, and agree to abide by this Code of Conduct.
This Code of Conduct is subject to change at the discretion of the Legal Design Summit Board. Any updates will be communicated to participants.

LDS Blog #8: Legal Design Summit 2025: what we learned (and what’s next for the community)
As we look back to the Legal Design Summit 2025, we feel renewed. It was not just an event, but a gathering of hearts and minds from all around the globe, willing to imagine systemic change in law. A group of dedicated and brave people can truly achieve anything – like building a 600+ person conference and community for social transformation on a non-profit basis. Our volunteer team was brilliant. Thank you to all speakers, partners, gallery contributors and participants, too: you made it happen as much as we did.
Shaking up the LDS format
This year, we dared to try new things and shook up traditions. We moved from purely user- and human-centric design to more-than-human justice, and legal design for sustainable futures, including AI governance, grass-roots-level democracy and innovative legal education. We reimagined the whole BrainFactory concept into a city-wide workshop sprint, and introduced the idea of co-creation, bringing new ideas and people together, in both the BrainFactory and the Summit program. We also launched the Gallery – a free online resource of legal design projects and concepts applied in practice. We will soon share how projects can continue to be submitted to the Gallery.
What we learned
We also learned a lot – about ourselves, about event organization, about creating an engaging program, and about community building. Thank you for all the feedback and reflections you have shared. Every Summit gives us new perspectives, and this year was no exception. Here’s our key learnings from this year:
Community first: We need to facilitate people coming together, matching thematically aligned people for collaboration, and thus build global networks for innovation, social change and meaningful, transformative careers.
Global reach: Inclusive representation is one of the most important backbones of the Summit. We aim to make the future even more inclusive and diverse with the help of regional chapters in bringing the Summit to life.
Impactful presentations: We recognize a need for engagement and critical thinking beyond traditional, individual keynotes. This might mean more co-created presentations that show the value of legal design in hands-on practice.
Value-based leadership: The Summit’s strength lies in shared principles. How can we turn our values – selflessness, inclusion, courage, and authenticity – into action in designing the evolution ana leading the revolution in law, both locally and globally?
Opening up new possibilities
Building on these key takeaways, we are excited to continue opening up possibilities for the Summit and the related global movement. The intertwined challenges we face – growing inequality, democratic decline and the triple planetary crisis – call for bold, creative, empathetic and interdisciplinary design-thinking. At the same time, legal design is still not properly embedded in legal education and practice. The wider challenge for the Summit is thus moving from vision to practice: defining what legal design means in action, finding ways to scale and measure its impact, and building the connective tissue between the community, its audience, and the work itself so that legal design becomes truly impactful in mainstream legal practice.
‘The ultimate, hidden truth of the world is that it is something that we can make, and could just as easily make differently’ (David Graeber, 2024).
Legal designers know this.
We also know that no one can really design alone. This crystallizes the Summit’s role as not only an event for knowledge sharing about legal design, but as a community and a platform for leading meaningful change in law. We feel hopeful for what’s ahead, knowing that we are creating it together.
This post was written by Sade Mäntylä, Mia Ihamuotila and Humzah Khan part of the Core Team for LDS 2025.

LDS Blog #6: BrainFactory 2025 - Expanding the Playground for Legal Design
#BrainFactory
For years, the BrainFactory concept has been one of the most loved parts of the Legal Design Summit: an immersive three-day experience where a small group of around 40–45 participants worked side-by-side with top facilitators from around the world to tackle real-life legal challenges
Over the years, we’ve heard more than once: “BrainFactory changed my life.” That kind of feedback is both humbling and energising — a reminder of the power of bringing people together in a space that sparks creativity, courage, and collaboration.
This year we asked: how might we make BrainFactory accessible to more people without diminishing opportunities for individual connection, creativity, and shared learning? What resulted is a bold leap: tripling the number of attendees by welcoming 125 participants into the experience across 9 workshops.
By expanding the number and variety of workshops, our goal is to significantly increase the number of people who get to experience and lead BrainFactory. More participants mean more perspectives, more diversity, and more accessibility. We believe that’s where the magic happens.
From a small lab to a city-wide experience
Instead of three days in one location, BrainFactory will unfold in a single, dynamic day across Helsinki. Nine workshops, each led by a unique team of facilitators, will dive into different corners of legal design. From AI to sustainability to access to justice, participants can explore specialized themes or start from the basics if they’re just beginning their journey.
This shift isn’t just about efficiency, although it helps participants who might only be able to take one more day off work. It’s about creating more intersections: more voices, more experiences, more chances for people to meet collaborators they’d never otherwise cross paths.
Co-creation at its core
While the BrainFactory spirit has always been co-creative, we wanted to extend that opportunity to the facilitators themselves. Some workshop teams are made up of long-time friends and past collaborators, already attuned to each other’s ways of thinking. Others are brand-new combinations, meeting for the first time through this process. In both cases, the result is a vibrant space where people who share a deep passion for legal design — and have been working on it professionally for years — come together to create something unique. Along the way, they’re learning from each other, exchanging methods, and inspiring one another in real time.
We sought out facilitators who could bring substance from different areas of legal design, from different areas of the world, ensuring the program reflects the diversity and depth of the field today. It all comes together thanks to sponsors who make the workshops possible and bring their own expertise to the table. We are incredible grateful to our sponsors in this process.
Always learning and evolving
As always, BrainFactory is a work in progress. We welcome feedback from every participant, so that in the future, it can grow in directions we can’t even imagine yet.

LDS Blog #2: From Proposals to Program - Creating the Legal Design Summit 2025
#Making of LDS
This post was written by Humzah Khan , part of the Legal Design Summit Program and Speakers Team.
Legal Design Summit program is live! In this blog we pull back the curtain and share how we created the program - from our approach to what we learned along the way. From ideas on sticky‑notes to a polished two‑day experience – a snapshot of our journey.
How we built the program
First, thank you to the brilliant team: Akvilė, Danielle, Katie, Marielle, Mia, Sade and Serena. For their collective effort over the last six months. It was a pleasure to work with a group of committed volunteers who, each in their own way, brought energy, creativity and curiosity to the process. Say hello and a big thank you when you see them in Helsinki! Building on the momentum of a remarkable 2023 Summit, we set out to do something bold for the fifth LDS: create an ambitious, advanced and future-looking program. To get there, we collaborated across the LDS team, embedded design thinking into our methods, and co-created with our community. Step-by-step, here’s how we built the program:
1. Brainstorming workshop
We kicked things off with a workshop with the LDS team to explore what our audiences needed and brainstorm possible themes. We mapped the legal design landscape through three lenses - the past, present, and future - and explored how those themes met the needs of different audiences, for example academics, practitioners, and participants new to the scene. One interesting thing we highlighted was the opportunity to create a stronger feedback loop between academic research and how legal design is being applied in practice. In addition to being good fun and a bit of an icebreaker for a new team, the workshop also gave us clear direction for the types of proposals we wanted to attract, highlighting themes from AI governance to access to justice to re-designing democracies. The workshop also identified opportunities for new content - this blog being one of them!
2. Call for speakers
We launched a public call for speaking proposals at the end of April 2025. This was open for one month and required submission of a Google form. The form included a series of questions, from motivations for speaking to a short summary of the proposal itself. We received over 70 proposals! The overall quality of the proposal was extremely high and we were impressed with the depth and breadth of the ideas and concepts. Thanks again to everyone who took the time to submit a proposal.
3. Reviewing proposals
To ensure fairness and quality, we designed a collaborative and robust review process. Every proposal was reviewed independently by at least two team members, using pre-agreed criteria and a shared scoring framework. We prioritised proposals that aligned with themes identified in the workshop and presented new, interesting, and original ideas. We welcomed proposals that were critical of legal design, as we highlighted this as an important intervention for the movement to continue building momentum. We dismissed proposals of a commercial nature. With 22 sessions to fill, we created an exhaustive shortlist of the top performing proposals. It took several brainstorming sessions to discuss the ideas and boil the number of proposals down. Making final decisions was definitely the hardest part of the process. In some cases, we noticed several strong proposals orbiting the same theme, so decided to combine their ideas into joint sessions.
4. Designing the program and confirming speakers
Once we had our shortlist, we started shaping the full program. We clustered the final set of proposals to find subtle narratives and identify themes that complemented each other. This was critical as we wanted to get the pacing and flow just right - making sure attendees would have a meaningful and engaging arc across the two days. Equally, we ensured a healthy balance between keynote speeches (20 mins) and fireside chats (30 mins), as well as coffee and lunch breaks. We confirmed the final speaker list at the end of May. Where we grouped proposals together, we scheduled calls to facilitate introductions, explain our rationale for creating joint sessions, and discuss their session. One team member commented that: “Creating fireside chats was one of our favorite parts of the process and we loved seeing speakers embrace the LDS spirit. One highlight was a panel meeting for the first time and immediately using a Miro board to find the common threads between their proposals and working collaboratively to brainstorm their session.”
What we created
We think we’ve created an exciting, innovative and diverse program. There are some familiar faces and, of course, returning legends. However, the majority of the program includes new people and perspectives ready to challenge and expand what legal design is and can be. Here’s the unfiltered breakdown across the two days:
Day 1 is pure legal design, with a focus on its role and value across the profession. We’ll look at how legal design is shaping policy making, transforming legal services and grappling with generative AI - the sector’s biggest disruptor.
Day 2 is about impact, and what legal design can do for people and the planet. The future of law is as much about social and nature value as it is about technology innovation, so sessions touch on big systemic issues, including sustainability, justice and education.
We also created something new: The LDS Gallery
We received so many incredible proposals this year, but unfortunately couldn’t fit everyone on the shortlist into the program. So we’re launching a new platform. A new space to showcase the important and inspirational work that the legal design community is shaping around the world. The gallery will be a digital exhibition, initially launched with a select group of contributors, with the aim of exhibiting legal design projects, experiences and case studies. The response so far has been very positive. When confirmed, one contributor said: “I’m excited by this new feature and truly appreciate the opportunity to showcase my work in this innovative format.” Our ambition is that the Gallery becomes the Louvre of Legal Design, and look forward to sharing the first iteration with you soon.
Final reflections - what we learned
This was less about building an event schedule and more about writing the next chapter in the legal design story
The quality, ambition and range of perspectives across the ecosystem is genuinely incredible and inspiring
Embedding design thinking into our approach was our most valuable tool
Thanks again to everyone involved - see you in Helsinki in September!
LDS Blog #1: The Legal Design Summit: What we stand for as a global movement
This post was written by Sade Mäntylä , a lawyer and political scientist, doctoral researcher, and Vice Chair at the Legal Design Summit.
#Selflessness #Inclusion #Courage #Authenticity
The Legal Design Summit began as a movement focused on the end-users of legal services. Thus, the initial focus of the event, founded in 2016, was on contract design, user-experience in various legal services, and the wider interlinks between technology and law.
While legal design is still about all of these things, over the years, the Summit and the associated movement and community have grown towards a more ambitious agenda. The aim is no longer simply making law more usable but redesigning the whole legal system . What started off as a user-centric revolution in law is today a globally significant non-governmental organization aiming for transborder solidarity, deliberative democracy, social innovation and practical justice outcomes.
To reflect this changing vision ahead of the 2025 event, it was time to clarify what the Summit stands for. As a community, the Legal Design Summit is committed to four core values: selflessness, inclusion, courage, and authenticity . In what follows, I will explain how each is reflected in our activities.
Selflessness
Selflessness means that we believe in collective action and support as the most important tools in achieving our shared goals. We see each other and the world with empathy and never put personal gain before the interests of the community.
The Summit, organized by volunteers and relying on self-funded speakers, is not about personal profit or monetary value, but about a value of a different kind: collective imagination for better futures. This is no longer limited to simply end-users or other human beings but encompasses non-human beings, the environment, and even the future of life on earth.
Inclusion
By being inclusive we strive to create a space where different perspectives and backgrounds come together, and where diversity is celebrated – both in organizers and speakers. While legal design is still dominated by lawyers from Western countries, both legal and non-legal professionals from all over the world are increasingly and importantly involved in the community.
By organizing the Summit free of charge and with a focus on globally relevant themes, we aim to foster an accessible, international and multicultural community where anyone can participate in shaping the future of law.
Courage
Courage means that reaching for the impossible is in our backbone. We are curious about the world and passionate about improving it by innovatively combining practical and academic knowledge. When faced with a problem to solve, we ask: how hard can it be?
This means that we do not steer away from wicked problems – be they functional or theoretical. Nor do we accept the status quo as it is. There is always room for improvement, and it is up to brave people to rise to the challenge.
Authenticity
Authenticity means that the Summit is dedicated to making a difference through creative, outside-the-box design thinking. We strive to make an impact by showing up as our authentic selves and by remaining honest and open about our core mission.
By welcoming anyone from any background into the community, we aim to expand possibilities for legal design across all areas of law and society. This means remaining open towards what legal design can be, as well as its limitations – while staying true to the common core of design-thinking.
The value of collective imagination in turbulent times
As our guiding values show, the Summit stands for an important message. The Summit is not about monetary value, even if it often translates into it – many fantastic careers have taken off in its wake. Instead, it is about scaling ideas for a better world. When a diverse group of open-minded people with different perspectives come together, the outcome is bound to be valuable. Yet the value of such an outcome cannot be expressed in monetary terms, as its primary aim is not to create profit. Instead, it creates a path for a world where well-being and social justice are a reality for more ‘users’ – both human and non-human.
One of my personal key learnings from being involved in the Summit for the past few years is that a group of dedicated and imaginative people working for the same end goal can achieve – if not anything, almost anything. As we look to the upcoming event and beyond, the Summit invites everyone to join this community towards a world that is more just by design .

The Legal Icon Dictionary
Aneta Baranowska
LinkedIn
About this work
Legal documents are notoriously difficult to understand due to their complexity, formal language, and abstract terminology. This creates a communication barrier between legal professionals and their clients, particularly non-lawyers who are often the intended recipients of these documents. Our work addresses this issue through the development of a visual tool: the Legal Icon Dictionary.
The Legal Icon Dictionary is a curated set of intuitive, standardized icons that represent key legal terms and concepts. It functions as a visual layer that can be integrated into legal documents - such as contracts, terms and conditions, and privacy policies - to support and clarify written content. The dictionary transforms abstract or ambiguous legal ideas into concrete, visual symbols that enhance comprehension and reduce cognitive load.
In co-creation of legal designers, graphic designers, and users, we mapped common legal concepts that are difficult to interpret and translated them into universally understandable icons. The process involved user research, iterative prototyping, and validation to ensure each icon conveyed its intended meaning. What inspired this project was a very practical challenge: even when lawyers want to make their documents more accessible by using visual elements, they often struggle to find the right tools. Searching through hundreds of icons online or trying to assemble visuals in office software can be frustrating and time-consuming. Our goal was not only to make documents easier to understand for end users, but also to make them easier to create for legal professionals. In its current format, the Legal Icon Dictionary consists of the:
1. report from our research
2. ready-to-use set of icons
3. and manual full of tips on how to use them – free for everyone to download and implement into their work. It reflects our commitment to demystifying the law through design, accessibility, and empathy.
Impact
The Legal Icon Dictionary has had an impact on both legal professionals and document recipients. By providing a ready-to-use, thoughtfully designed set of icons, we enabled lawyers - especially those taking their first steps into legal design—to create clearer, more user-friendly documents without the typical frustration of searching for visuals or struggling with formatting. We know of instances where the icons have been implemented into real-life legal documents, such as terms of service, privacy policies, and contracts. The icons helped clarify the structure and meaning of the text, resulting in more accessible and transparent communication for end users.
Beyond document design, the Icon Dictionary has become a valuable resource in our legal design workshops. We use it as both a practical tool and an educational example of how visual thinking can improve legal communication. It allows participants to experience first-hand how even small design interventions - like the addition of icons - can have a significant (good or bad, depending on the use!) effect on the document created.
By addressing the needs of both document creators and users, the Icon Dictionary continues to promote a more human-centered approach to law, lowering barriers to understanding and empowering legal professionals to innovate.
Key learnings
One of our key learnings was that visual tools can significantly enhance the accessibility of legal information - but only when used in the right context. While icons have the potential to clarify complex content, we discovered that without supporting context, users often interpreted them in unintended ways. This underlined the abstract nature of symbols: an icon on its own is rarely self-explanatory. Providing a clear use-case or embedding the icon within a familiar legal structure improved user understanding and reduced misinterpretation.
We also learned that even minor visual details - like the presence or absence of a magnifying glass - can dramatically alter how icons are perceived. Users tended to over-focus on specific graphic elements, leading to unexpected associations. As a result, icon design must be tested with real users and refined, as small changes can have a huge impact on usability.
A positive takeaway was that icons resembling familiar visual systems (e.g., from Office or mobile apps) were more easily understood. Leveraging users' existing visual literacy helped ground abstract legal concepts in something intuitive.

About this work
At OliveHarbor Global Solutions (OGS), we are developing Law & Tea—a community-based legal conversation series that brings everyday justice discussions into warm, welcoming spaces.
Many people—especially youth, women, informal workers, and migrants—don’t access legal support because traditional spaces feel intimidating or inaccessible. To respond, Law & Tea offers a low-cost, dialogue-based alternative, hosted in-person at local centres and cafés, and virtually via small Zoom circles. Each session features:
A real-life legal scenario or story
A visual “cheat sheet” or guide to simplify legal concepts
A trained facilitator to guide the discussion
A relaxed space where tea is shared and questions are welcomed
Topics range from housing disputes and job contracts to school rights and reporting abuse. The approach blends legal education, storytelling, and community care—without jargon or fear. The sessions are co-created with local youth groups and grassroots organisations in Kenya and Italy.

Still in the early pilot phase, we’ve hosted initial Law & Tea sessions in Nairobi (in-person) and Vicenza (virtually), reaching young people, community educators, and migrant leaders.

Designing for Justice: Lessons from CatholicCare’s Refugee Hub
Alice Neikirk
College of Human and Social Futures/ Newcastle Law School
LinkedIn
About this work
In 2023–24, a legal design project with CatholicCare’s Refugee Hub followed a five-step participatory design process, widely recognized in legal design practice. But as the project unfolded, we discovered something important: the standard approach doesn’t fully account for the realities of working with culturally and linguistically diverse communities.
Key insights from the project:
Time and patience matter. The design process often takes longer than expected—especially when building trust and understanding across cultures. Typical funding cycles may not allow for the flexibility needed. Culture shapes design. Cultural norms and communication styles influence how people engage with legal resources. These factors must be thoughtfully integrated into every stage of the process.
Service providers are essential partners. Rather than being peripheral, service providers should be central collaborators. Their insights and relationships with the community are vital for creating resources that truly work. Sustainability is a design step. To ensure long-term impact, the final step in the design process should focus on sustaining relationships and engagement. This means building strategies for ongoing collaboration and resource use beyond the project’s end.
By placing these considerations at the heart of legal design theory and practice—and adding “sustaining” as a formal step—we can create tools that better serve diverse communities and contribute meaningfully to a more accessible justice system. Our work has created specific resources but also further developed the legal design process.
Impact
Over 18 months we co-designed two printed resources to support the community's understanding of the Special Humanitarian Visa Process and Australia's driving laws. These were two areas where low legal literacy significantly impacting on people’s lives. For the visa, it meant people could not make informed decisions regarding the likelihood of success (less than 10%), or the waiting time (often decades). For the driving guide, it meant people were going to court to contest charges which led to a criminal conviction. Criminal convictions can mean they cannot become citizens.
We have hosted workshops in three different languages to determine if the visa resource improved legal literacy - and it did! People had a better understanding of the visa options available to them and the steps to apply for the visa. The resource has been translated into five key languages.
The traffic guide is used by four different organizations. This guide has been particularly useful for service providers who are not familiar with the laws, but provide legal guidance to clients.
Key learnings
The legal design process itself can increase the capacity of communities. It provides a forum for them to speak into the issues that impact on them, ask questions, and through co-creation, increase their ability to explain the issues to other people.
Good design takes time, especially when working with culturally and linguistically diverse communities. It is important to take the time to build a team who represents a variety of experiences and expertise. It takes time to build trust, to ensure people can speak into the process as equals. Respecting cultural norms, like Ramadan, mourning periods, or the need to travel internationally for extended periods, can also mean that the project will take more time. Time needs to be invested at the end as well, to ensure it continues to reflect the needs of the community.
During the translation process, we also learned that AI can be a powerful tool for simplifying complex language. However, it must also be used cautiously as it can change the meaning of the language.
Meet me at the conference
I would love to connect with other people doing co-design to support communities from migration backgrounds.
The next step of our design project is to transform parts of our written resources into reel-style videos. Particularly for the guide to driving in Australia, our younger audience does not find the printed resources (or virtual version) engaging. This is an exciting new challenge - how to translate key information into an accessible format that will appeal to younger people.

VTT
LinkedIn
About this work
I will introduce three different types of artefacts that I have created and leave it up to you to decide what part of it is legal design and what is closer to business or research.
1. Spectrum of IP protection meets AI as a hybrid asset: The first work is an illustration that combines legal and technical aspects relating to AI. The illustration aims to gap knowledge from two very different areas, legal and technical, in order to understand the interlinkages, challenges and opportunities embedded in the use of AI in society.
2. Business model innovation map for open assets: The second work is a business model innovation map that collects 27 open-source software (OS) business models, 5 business model categories from open data (OD) and 4 from open hardware (OH) into a holistic map. The map is meant to navigate through the multifaceted business model landscape of open assets.
3. Guidebook: Data ecosystems for smart sustainable cities: The third work is an interactive pdf-tool dedicated to the development of data ecosystems and the promotion of data sharing to advance smart city initiatives. Our objective was to collect guidelines, share valuable insights, and present practical examples to assist all stakeholders within a smart city in building data ecosystems and enhancing data sharing practices.
These works have been made in research projects ZOOOM (ZOOOM – 3Os and IP awareness raising for collaborative ecosystems (zooom4u.eu)) and DataMust ( Markets for Sustainable Cities - VTT's Research Information Portal ) funded by the European union and Business Finland.
Impact
All artefacts aim to give a diverse group of users easy access to complex knowledge that contains multidisciplinary elements, for instance legal, business and research aspects.
1. Spectrum of IP protection meets AI as a hybrid asset: With the visualization, the user can investigate questions relating to AI in a more structured manner – for instance, what kind of legal challenges arise with respect to different components involved or what kind of ethical questions may arise in different phases of the machine learning process.
2. Business model innovation map for open assets: A company can use the map to navigate through the multifaceted business model landscape of open assets. A company can explore the map to investigate the opportunities embedded in these business models and use it as a base for the innovation of its own business model.
3. Guidebook: Data ecosystems for smart sustainable cities: The guidebook outlines essential elements and phases involved in the process of building data ecosystems. A city itself or a company involved in smart sustainable city ecosystems can use it for building a clear vision, developing the necessary capabilities for forming, growing, and operating data ecosystems, adopting regulatory frameworks, and fostering collaborative innovation.
Key learnings
For me, one of the key learnings from the above work has been in understanding how design work intertwines with substance work, be it legal, business or other. Designers do not work merely to produce a final artefact, like the maps or guidebooks presented above. Visual thinking and design should be used also for the intermediate steps to enhance the substance thinking. This phase of the work is closely connected to substance knowledge and requires multidisciplinary capabilities, often several participants.
In legal design, this emphasises the need for constant, iterative discussions between legal experts and design experts. And, as legal design is there to resolve a problem for different types of users from several expert disciplines or technological fields, this iterative process requires interaction with several other fields.
This kind of multidisciplinary design process calls for different design methods and tools for different phases. One might use systemic design tools to understand the problem, for instance how business and legal issues interact, then turn into service design methods to increase understanding on the user and finally to produce an artefact that helps the user in resolving the problem at hand.

o What types of design methods and tools could be used in different phases of the design process – for instance service design, systemic design, tools for visual thinking and multidisciplinary exploration?

Scaling people-centered justice: A Justice Innovation Lab Approach
Zainab Malik
HiiL: Dedicated to People-centred Justice
LinkedIn
About this work
HiiL’s Justice Innovation Lab (JIL) addresses one of the world’s most urgent challenges: the justice gap. We know that justice problems, whether related to land, family, crime, or neighbour disputes, are common and have a serious impact on people’s lives. Yet, more than two-thirds of the world’s population still fail to find resolution. It’s clear that justice systems are not meeting people’s everyday needs.
This is where people-centred justice comes in. It’s an emerging approach that transforms justice systems by putting people and their justice needs at the centre, rather than institutional interests.
An integral tool for advancing this approach is the Justice Innovation Lab—a structured, design-thinking process that brings together a diverse group of stakeholders, including government leaders, judges, lawyers, community organisers, entrepreneurs, and users of justice. The process starts with data. Using insights from HiiL’s Justice Needs and Satisfaction (JNS) surveys and other research, the participants identify the most frequent and impactful justice problems people face. They then map user journeys to understand the barriers they encounter and use these insights to guide the co-creation of practical, locally grounded solutions. Over four to six months, co-create solutions, test ideas, prototype, build governance structures and sustainable financial models, and design pilot programmes ready for implementation. What makes the JIL unique is its deep commitment to local ownership, relevance and sustainability. In addition to formal justice actors, the Lab includes informal providers and people who have experienced justice problems firsthand. This broad participation ensures that both the design process and the solutions are not only innovative but truly people-centred.
Inspired by innovation labs in health, education, and finance, the JIL adapts this method specifically for the justice sector: one of the most under-innovated public services. It provides the structure, tools, and collaborative space needed to create justice services that are scalable, sustainable, and genuinely solve people’s justice problems.
Impact
One example of impact from our work was in Ethiopia, where over 7 million legal issues arise annually—most remaining unresolved. Many people rely on shimagillé, respected community elders, to mediate disputes. However, both this traditional system and the formal justice system have been overwhelmed by demand, highlighting the need for innovation.
In cooperation with the Federal Government of Ethiopia, HiiL’s Justice Innovation Lab process led to the development of the Community Justice Center (CJC) model. Drawing on the success of customary courts in Oromia and the trusted role of shimagillé, the CJCs integrate traditional dispute resolution with formal mechanisms such as case referrals. This hybrid approach reduces pressure on courts while preserving cultural values and aligning with international human rights standards.
In 2025, CJCs have been launched in Addis Ababa, Afar, and Sidama regions, showing strong government support and potential for scale. Community justice models developed by the Justice Innovation Labs are also currently being piloted in Ogun State and Imo State in Nigeria. This work is transforming access to justice—bringing it closer to the people who need it most.
Key learnings
A core learning from our work is that innovation in justice is not just about new tools or ideas : it’s about systems transformation. We learned that justice solutions must move from the fringes and form part of the key justice journeys people follow to resolve their justice problems. To truly deliver people-centred justice, we must create an enabling environment: incorporating innovations through legal and regulatory frameworks, policy planning, and budgets. For example, community-based services that link formal and informal systems can be highly effective—but only when their outcomes are recognised and supported by courts and institutions.
We also caution against the "app-ification" of justice. While tech can improve access, it can also deepen inequalities. In Uganda, where internet access is limited, an online justice tool developed through our JIL had to be delivered through kiosks staffed by community workers. In Tunisia, stakeholders combined online and offline tools to deliver doorstep legal support through apprentice lawyers and paralegals.
These lessons apply across sectors: real innovation requires inclusive design, system integration, and context-sensitive delivery. Data, collaboration, and enabling environments must be the foundation of all innovation.

I look forward to hearing from attendees how they have managed to move solutions from design to implementation, and what they have learned on this journey.

Laura Fauqueur | Alexandra Sabbe Ferri
Legal Shake | SAGAN Avocats
LinkedIn
About this work
L’Oréal’s HR Data Privacy team needed to communicate their Global Privacy Policy to employees worldwide. The original version,16 pages of legal language in Word format, was compliant but unreadable. The challenge: create clarity, foster engagement, and build trust across 90,000 employees, from factory to HQ.
Legal Shake and Sagan Legal Design partnered to tackle this through legal design. We conducted deep research on L’Oréal’s internal culture, employee profiles, digital usage, and HR compliance goals. We mapped stakeholders, built personas and empathy maps, and explored the user journey to identify friction points.
The key question driving our design brief: How might we show employees that taking control of their data helps them achieve their personal goals safely?
The result: an interactive, digital tool, designed in Figma and implemented on L’Oréal’s SharePoint. Its tone, structure and interface made complex information actionable, accessible on any device, in any country.
We added playful games to boost engagement and ensure understanding of the policy. Following the global rollout, we were entrusted with adapting the format for France’s Local Privacy Policy.
Building on this success, we designed a Consents Portal, giving employees clarity and control over the use of their personal data.
What started as a static document became a dynamic legal ecosystem. The approach proved scalable, desirable, and replicable, redefining how legal content can be communicated and trusted within global organisations.
Impact
The redesigned Global Privacy Policy was viewed by thousands of employees at launch, with immediate positive feedback. HR and legal teams saw a clear increase in understanding, interaction, and meaningful questions from employees about data use.
This success led to the localisation of the tool for the French market, then to the development of the Consents Portal. That platform is now live and used daily, empowering employees to manage their own data choices in real time.
Each tool is a legal product but also a cultural signal. Employees see that privacy isn’t imposed; it’s co-designed with them in mind. Legal and HR teams saw the added value: more transparency, fewer misunderstandings, stronger engagement.
Together, these tools form a strategic ecosystem that aligns legal compliance with a respectful, empowering employee experience redefining how legal content is communicated at scale.
Key learnings
This project proved that transforming legal understanding is not about simplifying law, but about designing for people. Legal design helped us bridge legal compliance and user engagement. Key takeaways:
• Empathy is strategy: Researching users’ needs and fears is essential for relevance and trust.
• Visual structure drives clarity: Layout, hierarchy, colour and navigation shape legal comprehension.
• Iteration builds quality: Each test cycle refined the clarity, usability, and visual coherence of the tools.
Our methods are widely transferable to other companies, industries, and jurisdictions particularly where digital user experience meets legal complexity (e.g., consents, terms, onboarding, internal policies).

Right now, our main concern is: How might we integrate more fun and more beauty into legal experiences?
We’d love to exchange ideas and stories about how you’ve brought fun and beauty into your own legal design work, what worked, what surprised you, what you’d try again.
We’re also keen to explore replicability: How might we maintain clarity and compliance across languages, cultures, and regulations within large organisations? And how might we redesign consents, rights requests, or onboarding experiences as part of a better digital legal UX?
We’re looking forward to hearing what others are working on and building new collaborations!

Caroline Muchina
Founder | Savannah Consulting

About this work
My work is a strategic and service-oriented framework titled Regulatory Compliance as a Service (RCaaS) , designed to tackle the complex, fragmented, and often inaccessible nature of regulatory compliance across institutions, particularly in Kenya and Africa. Positioned at the intersection of governance, regulation, and digital transformation, RCaaS offers a modular, human-centred approach to rethinking legal systems within private and public sectors.
Rooted in my role as Head of Legal at a leading public health training institution, and inspired by years of public service, I developed RCaaS in response to recurring compliance bottlenecks, slow policy execution, opaque bureaucratic processes, and the growing demand for digitised governance. My work has been shaped by the urgent need to demystify regulation for public institutions, ensuring laws and policies don’t just exist, but actually work for people.
The RCaaS framework combines:
Regulatory Compliance Advisory – licensing, statutory obligations
Risk & Compliance Management Consulting – risk registers, policy alignment
Legal Operations & Contract Management – CLM systems, automation workflows
Cybersecurity & Data Protection Advisory – best practices, vendor selection
Regulatory Change Monitoring – tracking legal reforms across Africa
The format is both a framework and a consultancy model, supported by technology and grounded in empathy. It offers structured yet adaptable solutions to help institutions modernize legal operations while improving transparency, and maintain regulatory readiness amid constant change.
The concept matured during my certification at MIT’s LegalTech in the Digital Era program, where I gained deeper insight into legal design, automation, and ethical tech use. RCaaS is ultimately a call to make law work better for people through intelligent, inclusive, and scalable design.
Impact
The RCaaS framework has helped bridge the gap between regulatory intention and real-world implementation in complex institutional environments. By integrating digital tools into legal and compliance processes, my work has enabled public institutions to reduce delays, improve transparency, and build a culture of proactive governance.
For example, in my current role, we digitised contract workflows, streamlined board governance using an eBoard system, and introduced compliance roadmaps that have reduced approval turnaround times by over 40%. This not only increased institutional efficiency but also improved stakeholder trust and accountability.
More broadly, the framework has sparked dialogue about how legal operations and regulatory adherence can be reimagined, not through more bureaucracy, but through clarity, accessibility, and human-centred technology. RCaaS is now informing cross-sector discussions on how to simplify compliance in areas like healthcare, education, and licensing, particularly in resource-constrained environments.
The most meaningful impact has been shifting legal culture: from reactive enforcement to strategic enablement. Institutions are beginning to see compliance not as a burden, but as a service; one that, when designed well, empowers both institutions and the citizens they serve.
Key learnings
Start small and show results. Legal transformation doesn’t have to be large-scale to be meaningful. Solving one pain point at a time builds trust, grows momentum, and easily earns you institutional buy-in.
Designing with, not for, users. Legal solutions must reflect the lived realities of those navigating them. Human-centred design is not just a technique, it’s a mindset.
Simplification is a superpower. The most effective compliance tools are the ones that make the law clearer, not more complicated.
Cross-functional collaboration is essential. The best outcomes come from working across disciplines; bringing together lawyers, policymakers, and users to co-create solutions.
Context is everything. What works in one jurisdiction or sector may fail in another. Localisation must be part of every legal innovation strategy.

Human OversAIght: the Ops Room
Joaquín Santuber
Research Group Lead | Metaverse Lab, JKU Linz
LinkedIn
About this work
HUMAN OVERSAIGHT: THE OPS ROOM is a real-time generative, interactive audio-visual installation developed as a human oversight department for high-risk AI systems. It is established according to the European Artificial Intelligence Act [EU AI Act], as described in Article 14, which suggests that in high-risk AI systems a human-machine interface such as a “stop button” is needed, to bring the system to a halt.
Can the system be stopped by a button, and why not? We investigate how can a human oversee and evaluate these processes, by visualising the internal operations of an automated system. We invite the visitors to take upon a central role in human oversight of high-risk AI systems and encourage them to engage with a human-machine interface to oversee the systems' operations. We provide possibilities for action for people to claim back their political agency to stop or affect the system.
Interaction
Participants are able to use a physical interface*, to navigate and control the visual input on the LED wall. The imagery shown on the wall is a mix of real-time surveillance input, including a face detection software, as well as synthetic imagery created via custom-made machine learning-based models.
While interacting, and navigating this content, the participants are directly affecting the algorithm, changing the content on the screen in real-time. Each activity is logged, irreversibly changing the system and its content. In such interactive feedback loop, we explore how quickly human input can affect a seemingly organised system, and change its rules. Besides the real-time visual output, we present the logs, which are anonymously stored.
Moreover, this installation presents the first human-computer interface for AI oversight and stop-button available to the public, as a tool for oversight, simulation, and agency.
Impact
The guiding question of the project is: How can people and communities regain their political power by engaging in human-oversight of Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems? More concrete: the EU AI Act proposes new concepts that leave many questions open to be answered—for example related to the human oversight of high-risk AI systems. Instead of leaving these questions to the experts, lawyers, engineers, programmers and designers, we believe people should give meaning to these abstract concepts by immediate experience and real-time interaction. The project offers embodied and situated ways for social participation in the definition of the AI legalities—what is legal-illegal, lawful-unlawful, right or wrong. In the HUMAN OVERSIGHT installation through an immersive, physical and embodied experience participants have the opportunity to engage with the first human-oversight interface designed for public participation, while constructing a meaningful socio-political understanding of EU AI regulation and the role they play in defining how they are affected by AI in their daily lives.
Key learnings
Human OversAIght takes an artistic approach to dealing with the questions of the law, while designing an oversight system. Working on this project with artists opened up new possibilities on how to translate a new legal institution into an aesthetic experience. As an artwork, it is not limited to the need to be functional or the required usability.
Our driving force is a deep belief and commitment that law and democracy offer the best alternatives to the monopoly of power and control by the Big Tech –HOWEVER our current legal systems need help in becoming more actionable, tangible, and closer to people. By making legal concepts and imaginaries tangible, people can interact and embody new roles that provides awareness and confidence to regain their political power about the limits and possibilities of AI—people have something to say and do about it, they just need the spaces to do so!
Making such processes and tasks experienceable to a general audience –people likely without any professional knowledge in technical or legal field– can expose a critical insight into specific and concrete challenges of navigating the role in the automated decision-making processes under human oversight, while enhancing social awareness and technical demands that are intersecting at the developed scenario in the EU AI Act being taken into action.

The Legal Design Summit Timeline
How did it all come to be?
The story of Legal Design Summit is a story of people who had great interest in legal design, & of how volunteering & the community are the backbone of all movements.
2016
The original birth of the Legal Design Summit
It all began with a vision...
The vision: Founders Antti Innanen & Johanna Rantanen (from Dottir Attorneys ) and Jaakko Wäänänen (from Hellon design consultancy), together with Pilvi Alopaeus (then a student at the U of Helsinki Law School) envisaged the first ever legal design event in Finland
The venue: Pilvi contacted Kimmo Nuotio (Dean of Law School at the U of Helsinki ) who offered the law school as the venue, making the first Legal Design Summit possible.
The organisers: The event was financed by ticket sales & by Dottir & Hellon , and organised by a group of students from Finnish Law Schools, led by Pilvi as the CEO.
The attendees: The event was national & had about 300 attendees (who paid for tickets). Speakers included legal design big names such as Margaret Hagan , Helena Haapio & Stefania Passera!
The impact: The event had a great impact on the Finnish legal tech & design field. As an example, the Legal Tech Lab of the U of Helsinki, led by Riikka Koulu , was created after the Summit.
The power of community: from a national event to the largest legal design event in the world and the first ever BrainFactory
2017
After the great impact of the first Legal Design Summit, the founders thought this could not be just a one off; there was something special here...
The vision: Antti called Pilvi & suggested she take the role of CEO & gather a team a volunteers, Hellon & Dottir would support, and Antti , Johanna & Jaakko would coach & guide whenever needed. Pilvi said yes in about 20 seconds and the next thing she did was to call 15 of her friends and ask: "should we create the largest legal design event in the world?”. They all said "yes". Soon the team included 50-100 volunteers that all believed in the same vision.
The venue: The venue: U of Helsinki & newly-founded Legal Tech Lab offered to sponsor the venue; the Great Hall of the U of Helsinki that would have seats for 700 people. The event was still to be commercially backed by Hellon & Dottir , however, the financing was based on ticket sales and sponsorships. Quite quickly the team realised that, in order to really create an impact and attract interest, the Summit should not be just an event, but an invitation for people to discuss with open minds, & make every participant feel ownership of the event and the community. In other words, they needed to spark a movement and a community around the Summit.
Building the community: There already was an amazing community on legal tech and design around the world, brought together by many great non-profit organisations and academic institutions and the Team wanted to bring them together.
The attendees: The Summit included over 700 international attendees, plus an amazing group of speakers such as Cat Moon , Dan Jackson , Margaret Hagan . The event was a success and the special atmosphere was described to be open, safe, international, and inviting. It was not just an event but a festival celebration of the community.
The impact: This was also the first year when BrainFactory was held. It was important to create a workshop where everyone could feel safe to try out the legal design method when guided by legal design professionals, while working on real life cases in small teams. It was also important for the team to harness the power of diversity as much as possible. The cases were provided by real clients, including but not limited to the Finnish Ministry of Justice . The BrainFactory was a great success, and many wonderful new careers began as a result of it.
2019
Re-founding the Legal Design Summit as a non-profit organisation; The new beginning as a free event
The Legal Design Summit of 2017 had been a great success. It had ignited the community and created joy, new opportunities, and paths for many...
The vision: In early spring 2019, Jukka Linna , who led the Laurea University of Applied Science’s legal design studies, wanted to make a new Summit happen. Thus, Jukka ( U of Applied Sciences ), Mika Viljanen ( U of Turku ), Henna Tolvanen , Nina Toivonen , and Pilvi Alopaeus came together to consider what to do with the Summit. Even though the Summit of 2017 was a success, there had been something missing; on paper, it was still owned by Dottir & Hellon , but the community had taken ownership of it:
The Three Decisions:
The first decision: was to found the "Legal Design Finland Association" as a legal entity led by Jukka , Mika , Nina , Henna & Pilvi . Dottir and Hellon graciously gave all ownership to it in order to support the community and the legal design movement. Their support had been paramount over the years but they agreed that it was time for the Summit to try out its own legs & see where the community would take it.
The second decision: that shaped the fate of the Summit was to make the Summit free of charge. This would really make the event more accessible. When word of this decision reached the global community, the reception was overwhelmingly positive. The Summit had taken probably one of its most important steps to date.
The third decision: was that the Summit would not become a digital event . The world is full of amazing digital events but the Summit would be about meeting people in real life. The Board understood that this would limit the accessibility of the Summit, but they were confident that this would be necessary for the event’s spirit. The most important part, that the Summit was organised and would always be organised by volunteers would remain as the core value of the organisation. Thus, the amazing volunteer team of 2019 was formed. Pilvi continued as the CEO and Henna and Nina took the hats of Co-Producers .
The venue and funding: The City of Helsinki kindly allowed the Summit’s venue to be the City Hall of Helsinki. The event was funded fully by sponsorships.
The organisers: The team also collaborated with several amazing non-profit organisations & academic institutions in order to bring the community together.
The attendees: The program was a great success and included the super stars of legal design and many new voices and ideas. The event was more international than ever before: people came from all over the world - every continent was represented! The spirit was described to be a "Woodstock-like”.
The impact: The BrainFactory was organised at Maria 01 (the Nordic’s leading startup campus). Real cases were provided by real clients, such as the Finnish Ministry of Environment . The BrainFactory was again a huge success and had a great impact on many individuals who took part in it.
The Covid years...
2020 / 2022
In 2022, Noora Rissanen , Pilvi Alopaeus , Sonja Kojo , Juulia Lehtinen , and Malin Männikkö came together and formed the new Board of the owner association. The former Board members Jukka Linna , Mika Viljanen , Nina Toivonen , and Henna Tolvanen became the first honorary members of the Association.
The new Board started to think: should the Summit make a return? COVID19 had changed the world & also caused the Summit to have a long break. The Board wondered: would there still be a need for an event like Summit? Would the community still want to gather in person or would it be time to announce that the Summit would retire?
2023
Going global! The most international & diverse Legal Design Summit yet!
The Board decided to take a leap of faith and, in early 2023, the Volunteer Team of 2023 took the reins. For the 2023 edition, the volunteer team was more global and diverse than ever before! Organically, the theme of organising the most diverse Summit was born.
2023 saw some 600 participants from our 40 countries come together for the first time for a 2-day conference!
BACK AGAIN!
2025
In 2024, Mia Ihamuotila , Sade Mäntylä , Andrea Lindblom , Marika Nieminen , and Malin Männikkö formed the new Board of the Legal Design Summit Association - and began the work for the 2025 Summit. The team set out to recruit a even more global volunteer team than before - stay tuned for the results!
